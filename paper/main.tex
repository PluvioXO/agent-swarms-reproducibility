\begin{abstract}
Evaluating an agent swarm requires distinguishing the effects of increasing
the total token budget, dividing a fixed ceiling, and changing coordination.
We examine these comparisons in 8,400 controlled trials on fresh arithmetic
and graph-colouring tasks. At 4,096 completion tokens per worker, increasing
independent width from one to four improves accuracy by 16.0 percentage
points on both families while the total ceiling quadruples. Splitting a
fixed 4,096-token completion ceiling instead lowers graph accuracy from
56.0\% to 18.5\%; the arithmetic change is inconclusive. The primary comparisons
do not establish an accuracy advantage over a sequential control matched
on scheduled calls and nominal token ceiling. Ring communication provides no
consistent gain. Eleven complementary scaling studies add 20,376 held-out
trials across seven task families, including a fresh-task sixteen-worker
confirmation that does not establish superiority over fixed-four execution.
Candidate and request audits show that finding a valid answer after an initial
failure depends on the token allowance. They also reveal incomplete worker
activation and 97 controlled-study attempts with unknown usage.
The findings show how resource controls, output selection, and execution
records qualify claims about agent scaling. They support evaluating specific
allocation policies; equal-realized-compute advantages and general scaling
laws remain unestablished.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Adding agents changes more than the number of contributors. Under a shared
token ceiling, wider systems receive smaller per-agent allowances, generate
more candidates, and may use different amounts of the available budget; where this may be tokens, time or reasoning.
A nominal worker can also fail to become active. An observed accuracy difference
therefore measures the deployed allocation and selection procedure, not an
isolated effect of collaboration.

This paper asks what fixed budget worker-count evaluations can establish
about task success, resource efficiency, and communication. We examine
8,400 controlled trials on fresh arithmetic and graph-colouring tasks,
alongside eleven complementary studies on programming, logical reasoning,
graph colouring, arithmetic search, and planning. Three distinctions organize the
analysis: a ceiling versus realized token use; requested versus active workers;
and improvement over one worker versus improvement over a strong fixed-width
baseline. These distinctions matter even when task grading is deterministic.

Our contribution combines controlled allocation and coordination comparisons
with an empirical audit of saved configuration grids, including negative
results, feasibility diagnostics, and a fresh-task policy confirmation. We provide all 176 held-out cells in
the repository, retain repeated tasks as statistical clusters, and make the limits of
the token ledger explicit. The resulting reporting recommendations are
derived from the audit; they have not themselves been validated as an
evaluation standard.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}
Repeated sampling separates solution coverage from successful candidate
selection \citep{brown2024monkeys}; self-consistency and debate provide
different aggregation and interaction mechanisms
\citep{wang2023selfconsistency,du2024debate}. Effective inference allocation
depends on task difficulty \citep{snell2024compute}, and controlled agent-system
studies already show dependence on architecture and task structure
\citep{kim2025agents}. We do not claim task dependence as a new principle.
Shared-workspace communication can help discovery tasks given sufficient
resources and feedback \citep{park2026discovery}; our single-round ring
comparison tests a narrower intervention. Failure taxonomies
\citep{cemri2025failures} complement our resource and activation diagnostics,
which do not by themselves identify a causal failure mechanism.

\paragraph{Controlled allocation comparisons.}
The controlled study comprises 8,400 trials on fresh arithmetic and
graph-colouring tasks at widths 1, 2, and 4. Fixing $d$ increases $B$ with
width; fixing $B$ reduces each worker's allowance. Independent workers revise
their own drafts; ring workers receive predecessor information. The
sequential control uses one worker for the same $2N$ scheduled calls and
nominal completion ceiling, with prior-candidate and verifier feedback.
All scheduled calls execute, and the first valid candidate is retained.
The repository contains the supplied protocol summary and states the
coverage of the available experimental records.

\paragraph{Evidence and task coverage.}

\paragraph{Interventions and selection.}
The original programming and ZebraLogic grids evaluate $N\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}$
at three ceilings. The five smaller diagnostic studies compare one and four
workers at two ceilings. The confirmation evaluates a transferred policy
and its controls. Workers draft and revise; ring revision adds a predecessor's
draft to the revision context. Independent revision omits that peer draft.
Both conditions can still share a final integrator in the programming and
ZebraLogic workflows. Thus the contrast does not remove every form of
information sharing.

Graph colouring, Countdown, and PlanBench select a locally valid revision
when available. Their width effects include verifier-assisted parallel search.
BBEH instead uses gold-blind plurality with deterministic tie breaking.
Programming and ZebraLogic use a model integrator, with final grading outside
the generation loop. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} records allocations and graders.

\paragraph{Outcomes and resource accounting.}
For a complementary study, nominal ceiling $B$, width $N$, and revision condition $c$, let
$\widehat S_{N,c}(B)$ denote mean final-task success and $\overline T_{N,c}(B)$
mean recorded tokens. We compare each wider cell to its shared $N=1$ control:
\begin{equation}
\Delta S=100\bigl(\widehat S_{N,c}(B)-\widehat S_1(B)\bigr),\qquad
R_T=\frac{\overline T_{N,c}(B)}{\overline T_1(B)}.
\label{eq:contrasts}
\end{equation}
$\Delta S$ is in percentage points (pp). $T$ retains each study's charged-token
or retained-debit convention, including conservative debits where applicable.
It is not a complete ledger of all attempted calls or provider compute.
Equal ceilings do not imply equal realized resources. Original calibration
also contains disclosed accounting overruns; nominal ceilings are not a
claim of universally verified hard caps (Appendix~\ref{app:accounting}).
These charged-token and retained-debit ceilings include prompt accounting;
Figure~\ref{fig:worker-schedules} instead specifies a completion-token ceiling
for the controlled schedules.

\paragraph{Uncertainty and analysis units.}
Cells share tasks, repetitions, and controls. Paired bootstrap intervals
resample whole tasks, keeping their observations together and using the
study-specific strata (Appendix~\ref{app:inference}); detailed resampling
settings are recorded in the repository.
The 26,648 trials are not independent task samples. Configuration counts
describe the evaluated grids; they do not estimate a population frequency
or constitute 148 independent hypothesis tests. Reported 95\% intervals are
study-specific and are not adjusted for cross-study multiplicity.

\section{Findings and Their Evaluation Consequences}
\label{sec:findings}

\subsection{Controlled allocation and coordination}
The controlled comparisons separate increasing the total allowance from
dividing a fixed ceiling (Table~\ref{tab:controlled-summary}). Increasing
independent width at a fixed per-worker allowance improves accuracy while
expanding the total ceiling. Under a fixed total ceiling, graph accuracy
falls sharply; the arithmetic comparison is inconclusive. These are
comparisons of allocation rules, not evidence of equal-realized-compute gains.

\subsection{A shared ceiling does not equalize resource use}
All 148 wider held-out cells use more recorded tokens than their
matched-ceiling control. Accuracy is lower in 75, tied in 24, and higher in
49 (Figure~\ref{fig:evidence}A). Thus 99 cells have higher recorded token use
without a higher mean success rate. Programming contributes 96 wider cells,
81 of them flat or worse; the overall count reflects unequal grid sizes and
must not be read as a benchmark-balanced effect.

The finding is a joint statement about observed accuracy and the recorded
resource ledger. It does not establish population-level dominance for every
cell, nor does it compare policies at equal realized expenditure. For
example, independent four-worker Countdown gains 9.5--17.0 pp, but uses
2.77--3.85 times the control's recorded tokens. Reporting only accuracy at
the shared ceiling would omit that resource difference.

\subsection{Width can cross a feasibility boundary}
On the PlanBench subset, independent four-worker accuracy is 0\% at
$B=8{,}192$, versus 95\% for one worker. At $B=16{,}384$, the same widths
score 100\% and 89.5\%. Their paired differences are $-95.0$ pp
($[-98.0,-91.5]$) and $+10.5$ pp ($[6.0,15.5]$). Four-worker length-limit
finishes fall from 99.94\% to 0.19\% of retained stage calls.
The higher-ceiling gain uses 4.06 times the control's retained debit.

BBEH provides a complementary case without verifier-assisted selection:
ring four-worker accuracy changes from a 6.5-pp loss ($[-11.5,-2.0]$) to
a 4.5-pp gain ($[0.5,9.0]$) when its ceiling doubles. Conversely,
graph-colouring four-worker cells lose 7.5--77.5 pp across both models and
ceilings. Muse's graph-colouring scores include seven operational failure
zeros at the higher ceiling (Appendix~\ref{app:accounting}).
The appropriate interpretation is conditional feasibility under
the tested allocation. Width and per-worker allowance change together;
lower truncation accompanies the reversals but is not isolated as their cause.

Requested width also differs from recorded participation
(Figure~\ref{fig:evidence}C). Pooling revision conditions, BigCodeBench Muse's
sixteen requested workers average 7.3\% activation at the smallest ceiling, rising to
45.3\% and 93.5\% at the next two ceilings. A worker-count label therefore
does not identify a stable amount of realized participation. This diagnostic
does not establish that activation alone explains accuracy.

\subsection{Sampling gains do not establish communication gains}
Countdown improves with independent workers while ring revision reduces
average accuracy by 5.0 pp ($[-8.5,-1.75]$). Luna graph colouring also has a
negative ring contrast, $-2.0$ pp ($[-4.0,-0.25]$). Most other communication
intervals include or touch zero (Appendix~\ref{app:inference}). PlanBench's
saved stratified interval is degenerate because its only differing task
occupies a singleton stratum; an unstratified sensitivity interval spans
$[0.0,0.75]$ pp. It is not evidence of general communication superiority.

These contrasts concern the tested ring intervention. Verifier-assisted
candidate selection, model integration, and peer exchange are distinct
mechanisms. A wider system's gain cannot be assigned to collaboration merely
because multiple workers contributed candidates.

\subsection{Fresh tasks require a strong baseline as well}
The original, post-start-amended ZebraLogic study reports a selected-policy
gain of 3.78 pp ($[1.30,6.51]$) against its calibration-selected fixed-width
baseline, which selected one worker. The confirmation transfers the
communicating $4/8/16$ policy without refitting to 300 unused puzzles and
compares it with communicating fixed four. Averaged over the two higher
ceilings, adaptive minus fixed-four accuracy is $+0.92$ pp
($[-0.58,2.50]$).

The original and confirmation contrasts have different comparators and
cannot be pooled. The confirmation establishes neither superiority nor
equivalence. Fresh tasks address task reuse, while a competitive fixed-width
control addresses whether adaptation adds value. Both checks are necessary
before interpreting a selected allocation rule as an established advantage.

\section{Reporting Implications}
\label{sec:reporting}
The audit motivates reporting ceilings and realized use, requested and active
workers, per-stage allowances, selection and feedback access, comparator
policies, task counts, and paired uncertainty alongside the final score.
A structured reporting checklist is provided in the repository. These are
practical requirements for interpreting this class of comparison, not an
empirically validated new metric.



\begin{samepage}
Further experiments should compare accuracy and realized resource use
while reporting width, per-worker allowance, selection, and
feedback access. Frozen adaptive policies should then face both
single-worker and fixed-width controls on new tasks with matched realized
resources. A larger ceiling alone is insufficient to make that comparison.
\end{samepage}

\section{Limitations and Reproducibility}
The studies are heterogeneous, use unequal grids, and are not replications
from independent laboratories. Programming samples contain only 16--32
evaluation tasks per study. Public-task exposure and unknown training
contamination limit generalization. Deterministic grading does not establish
that a task subset represents deployed agent work. Most protocols are short
draft--revision workflows rather than long-horizon, tool-using environments.

Model versions, provider routes, tier verification, and historical accounting
differ. Usage from lost calls and retry expenditure can be unknown.
The original ZebraLogic study and confirmation include disclosed operational
amendments; the evidence is not uniformly preregistered. Several pilot
truncation warnings were acknowledged before the frozen diagnostic grids
continued. We retain those failures rather than selecting only feasible cells.
No scaling exponent, exact compute comparison, latency advantage, or causal
effect of truncation is established.

The appendices retain essential protocols, interval definitions, and
interpretation caveats for the eleven complementary studies. Their extended
methods, recorded configurations, complete evaluation-cell table, saved
outcomes, provenance hashes, and aggregate verifier are in a versioned public
repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/PluvioXO/agent-swarms-reproducibility}}
The repository also provides the controlled-study protocol and result
summary, with an explicit coverage statement for its supporting records.
The current materials support saved-summary verification, not complete
inference replay or independent grader validation. No new model runs were performed for this
reframing.

\section{Conclusion}
Worker-count comparisons are interpretable only with their allocation,
participation, selection, and baseline conditions. The controlled study
separates total-budget expansion from division of a fixed allowance and
does not establish an advantage over matched sequential execution. The
complementary studies show that extra workers can consume more recorded
tokens without improving accuracy, budget changes can reverse width effects,
and a transferred policy can remain inconclusive against fixed four.
These findings support evaluation of specific allocation policies;
equal-realized-compute advantages and general scaling laws remain unestablished.
