\begin{abstract}
Evaluating an agent swarm requires distinguishing the effects of increasing
the total token budget, dividing a fixed ceiling, and changing coordination.
We examine these comparisons in 8,400 controlled trials on fresh arithmetic
and graph-colouring tasks. At 4,096 completion tokens per worker, increasing
independent width from one to four improves accuracy by 16.0 percentage
points on both families while the total ceiling quadruples. Splitting a
fixed 4,096-token completion ceiling instead lowers graph accuracy from
56.0\% to 18.5\%; the arithmetic change is inconclusive. The primary comparisons
do not establish an accuracy advantage over a sequential control matched
on scheduled calls and nominal token ceiling. Ring communication provides no
consistent gain. Eleven complementary scaling studies add 20,376 held-out
trials across seven task families, including a fresh-task sixteen-worker
confirmation that does not establish superiority over fixed-four execution.
Candidate and request audits show that finding a valid answer after an initial
failure depends on the token allowance. They also reveal incomplete worker
activation and 97 controlled-study attempts with unknown usage.
The findings show how resource controls, output selection, and execution
records qualify claims about agent scaling. They support evaluating specific
allocation policies; equal realized compute advantages and general scaling
laws remain unestablished from this work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Evaluation Design}
Worker-count evaluations combine resource allocation, candidate selection,
and communication. Prior work on sampling and inference allocation motivates
separating these factors \citep{brown2024monkeys,snell2024compute}.

The controlled study contains 8,400 trials on fresh arithmetic and
graph-colouring tasks at widths $N=1,2,4$. With per-worker completion
allowance $d$, total ceiling $B=Nd$, and two calls per worker, each call
receives $d/2$ tokens. Independent workers revise their own drafts; ring
workers receive predecessor information. A sequential control uses one
worker for the same $2N$ scheduled calls and nominal ceiling, with
prior-candidate and verifier feedback. All scheduled calls execute; the
first valid candidate is retained.

Eleven complementary studies contribute 20,376 evaluation and 6,272
calibration trials across seven task families. They use programming benchmarks
\citep{zhuo2024bigcodebench,austin2021mbpp,liu2023evalplus},
ZebraLogic \citep{lin2025zebralogic}, BBEH \citep{kazemi2025bbeh},
PlanBench \citep{valmeekam2023planbench}, and custom graph and Countdown tasks.
The programming and original ZebraLogic grids test widths 1, 2, 4, 8, and
16; the smaller diagnostic grids compare one and four. Generated graph
and arithmetic tasks use deterministic constraint checks. Selection varies
between verifier-assisted search, model integration, and gold-blind
plurality. Paired bootstrap analyses retain repeated observations within
task clusters; intervals are study-specific without cross-study adjustment.

All 148 wider complementary-study cells use more recorded tokens than their
matched-ceiling single-worker controls: accuracy is lower in 75, equal in
24, and higher in 49. These are descriptive counts from unequal grids
sharing tasks and controls.

PlanBench illustrates how the width effect can reverse with the allowance.
At an 8,192-token ceiling, four independent workers score 0\% versus 95\%
for one; at 16,384, they score 100\% versus 89.5\%, using 4.06 times the
single-worker retained debit. Width and per-worker allowance change together,
so the reversal does not isolate a causal mechanism.

A fresh-task ZebraLogic confirmation transfers a ring $4/8/16$ policy to
300 unused puzzles; against fixed four at the two higher ceilings, its
accuracy difference is $+0.92$ pp (95\% interval $[-0.58,2.50]$).
This establishes neither superiority nor equivalence.

\section{Interpretation and Reproducibility}
Equal nominal ceilings do not establish equal realized compute. Retained
charges are incomplete attempt ledgers; the controlled study includes
97 attempts with unknown usage. Recovery after initial failure depends on
the allowance. Heterogeneous models, small programming task sets, operational
amendments, and possible public-task contamination limit generalization.
The findings support evaluating specific allocation and selection policies;
general scaling laws remain unestablished.

Extended methods, all 176 complementary evaluation cells, saved outcomes,
recorded configurations, provenance, and an offline verifier are available
in the repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/PluvioXO/agent-swarms-reproducibility}}
The controlled-study summary includes an explicit evidence-coverage note.
The verifier covers the eleven complementary studies, without recomputing
intervals, validating graders, or replaying inference.
